# Historical Reference: What 1888 New York Looked Like

This research backs the game's environments. Colors are rendering estimates. Points marked [unverified] had thin evidence. The scales are compressed in the game (see "How the game simplifies" at the end).

## Steerage (1880s steamships)
- **US Passenger Act 1882:** a deck of 6 ft or more; berths **no more than 2 tiers**, each 6 × 2 ft, with 2 ft 6 in above each tier and the lower berth 6 in off the floor.
- **Berths:** iron frames with iron-pipe partitions (older ships used rough boards), set in long rows or blocks. One compartment could hold 300+ people.
- **Bedding:** sacks of straw, seaweed or wood shavings in white canvas or gingham; one grey or brown wool blanket; a cork life preserver for a pillow.
- **The hold:** plank floors strewn with sawdust, thin iron columns holding up the deck beams, and dim kerosene lanterns. Electric light was rare (Cunard's *Servia* of 1881 had it, mostly in first class).
- **Meals:** no dining room. Passengers ate from their own tin plate, cup and basin, at shelf-tables, on boards set on trestles, in their berths, or on deck. Stewards ladled food from big tin pans.
- **Layout:** single men usually forward; single women and families in separate compartments.
- **To the deck:** a steep, nearly vertical ladder up through the hatch.

## Castle Garden (immigrant depot 1855 – April 18, 1890)
- **The fort:** a former fort about **56 m (184 ft) across**, with one straight side making up about 1/8 of the circle, and red-brown sandstone walls 8 ft thick.
- **The roof:** rebuilt after the **1876 fire** as a low dome with a central lantern and a flagpole (38-star flag). The gallery was **never rebuilt**.
- **Inside:**
  - an open wooden rotunda, with benches around the room (also used as beds)
  - clerks' desks behind wooden railings
  - money exchange, railroad tickets, baggage, and an information bureau
- **Outbuildings:** brown-painted wooden buildings, including the Labor Exchange (80 × 50 ft, with a skylight). A 12–13 ft fence ran around the depot.
- **Arrival:** immigrants came by **barge** to a pier.
- **The Statue of Liberty:** dedicated October 28, 1886, 2.84 km to the SW on **Bedloe's Island**, on Fort Wood's **11-point star**.
  - In 1888 it was **copper brown**, not green; the green came around 1900–1906.
  - It worked as a lighthouse until 1902.

### Castle Garden details added from 1871–1880 accounts
- **Island fort:** built 1808–1811 on rocks off the tip of Manhattan and reached by a timber causeway. Landfill joined it to the Battery in the 1850s, so in 1888 only the harbor side faces water.
- **Walls:** square brownstone blocks about six feet thick, with the old gun ports still open. The nail-studded fort gates were still hung but left open.
- **Inside (documented):** registry desks, a railroad ticket counter, exchange brokers with a blackboard of the day's rates, a telegraph office, a desk where a clerk wrote letters for free, a bread stand (bread, milk, coffee, sausage), an iron staircase to the offices, and a blackboard of arriving steamers.
- **Outside (documented):** a hospital shed by the water entrance, customs sheds with baggage scales at the dock, a tall board fence, and barges towed by a tug.
- **Guesses in the game:** the exchange rates on the blackboard, the sign wording, the pot-belly stoves, and the roof color are plausible but not confirmed by a source.

### Castle Garden as rebuilt from period pictures (2nd environment pass)
- **Inside, from the 1860s–80s engravings:** a ring of slender iron columns with flared ("palm") capitals carries a raised **clerestory lantern** of arched windows in the middle of the roof. White picket railings divide the floor into aisles and pens. Booths are labeled **REGISTRY, EXCHANGE OFFICE, RAIL ROAD DEPARTMENT, INFORMATION**, and a two-story **Office of the Commissioners of Emigration** with an outside stair stands against the wall.
- **Outside, from the ca. 1880–90 aerials and Shults's 1880 landing view:** low, pale, gabled wooden sheds (not dark brown), a covered landing shed on the pier, and barges with long curved roofs. The hospital and customs sheds stand on a timber wharf at the water side.
- **The Battery, from ca. 1885 photographs:** a granite seawall with an iron pipe railing, gas lamps, slat benches, gravel walks, and small octagonal pavilions.

## Lower East Side
- **Hester Street, west to east:** Bowery (Third Ave El overhead), Chrystie, Forsyth, Eldridge, **Allen (Second Ave El overhead, opened 1880, stations at Canal and Grand)**, Orchard, Ludlow, Essex.
- **Block sizes:** blocks were about 200 ft. Side streets were about 50 ft wide building to building (a 30 ft roadway and 10 ft sidewalks). The Bowery was about 100 ft wide.
- **North to south:** Delancey, Broome, Grand, **Hester**, Canal, Division.
- **Neighborhood Guild (1886):** 146 Forsyth St, between Delancey and Rivington.
- **Eldridge Street Synagogue (1887):** 12 Eldridge St, between Canal and Division.
- **Chinatown:** Mott St from Chatham Square to Bayard, plus Pell and Doyers. **Church of the Transfiguration** is at 25 Mott (a Catholic parish since 1853).
- **Hester Street pushcart market:** from about 1886.
- **Language mix:** in 1888 the area was still partly German ("Kleindeutschland"), so signs were in English, German, and Yiddish.
- **Tenements:**
  - 25 ft lots, 5–6 stories, 4 window bays, red-orange brick
  - brownstone lintels and sills, and pressed galvanized-iron cornices with brackets
  - 2-over-2 sash windows, a shop on the ground floor
  - front iron fire escapes with **vertical ladders**
- **Apartments:**
  - The pre-1879 kind (e.g., 97 Orchard St, 1863): 4 apartments per floor, each about 325 sq ft. They had a front room about 11 × 12.5 ft, a kitchen, and a windowless bedroom about 8 × 8 ft. Privies were in the yard.
  - The **"dumbbell"** kind (after 1879) added narrow air shafts.
- **Streets:**
  - granite Belgian-block paving, bluestone sidewalks, manure in the gutters
  - cast-iron **gas lamps** about 9–10 ft tall with a ladder bar and a 4-sided glass lantern; the curved "bishop's crook" came later
  - pushcarts with 2 large wooden wheels, a flat bed about 3 × 6 ft, and long handles

### Inside a tenement flat (Riis photographs, 1888–89; 97 Orchard St)
- **Front room as workshop:** treadle sewing machines on a table under the window, heaps of cut coat pieces on the floor and chairs, finished coats piled high. The light came from a kerosene lamp.
- **Kitchen:** a cast-iron coal range with its stovepipe into the chimney, a kettle and pot, a sad-iron heating for pressing seams, a coal scuttle, and a washtub with a washboard. A shelf over the stove held brass Sabbath candlesticks.
- **Bedroom:** no window, or one onto the air shaft. A straw tick on the floor, the family's steamer trunk, and clothes on a line.
- **Halls:** almost dark. One gas jet for the whole floor, if any.
- **Street:** granite curbs, dark gutters, and manure, straw, and puddles between the paving blocks. Chimney stacks line the party walls on the roofs.

## Park Row and City Hall Park
- **The park:** a triangle with its point to the south (Broadway on the west, Park Row on the east).
  - **Mullett's Post Office** (granite, mansard roofs) fills the southern tip.
  - **City Hall:** white marble; the rear wall was brownstone painted white.
  - **Tweed Courthouse:** just north of City Hall; marble with a Corinthian portico, and in 1888 a grand stair on its north (Chambers St) side.
- **Across Park Row:**
  - **Tribune** (1875, red brick and granite, 260 ft clock tower) at Nassau & Spruce
  - **Times** (41 Park Row) **under construction in 1888**: a 13-story tower built around the old 5-story building while the presses kept running
  - **Potter Building** (1886, red brick and orange terra cotta, 11 stories)
  - The World Building wasn't built yet (1890).
- **Brooklyn Bridge** (1883): grey granite towers with twin pointed arches, buff-colored cables, and a terminal on Park Row.
- **Els:** the Third/Second Ave El City Hall branch ran along Park Row.
  - Lattice-iron girders, wooden gabled stations.
  - Maroon varnished wooden cars with gold trim, pulled by small black Forney steam engines.
- **Streetcars:** horsecars painted in each line's own color; Broadway horsecars from 1885.
  - **No electric trolleys** in Manhattan in 1888.
  - Cable cars were first tested on Tenth Ave in 1885.
- **Wires:** 1888 was the peak of the overhead **"wire forest"** of telegraph poles. The city started cutting them down in 1889.

### Inside the Tweed Courthouse
- **The rotunda:** octagonal, under a skylight, ringed by tiers of round arches in **red and cream brick** (Leopold Eidlitz's later work on the building) with cast-iron gallery railings. Courts and county offices opened off the galleries.
- **What it cost:** about **$13 million** by 1872, against a first budget of about $250,000. In July 1871 *The New York Times* printed the county's secret accounts. Among the bills:
  - Andrew J. Garvey, plasterer: **$133,187 for two days of work**
  - a carpenter: **$360,751 for one month**
  - three tables and forty chairs: **$179,729**
- **In the game:** framed copies of these bills hang on the rotunda wall ("What This Building Cost"). Plunkitt's bootblack stand is inside the building, where he actually held court. The naturalization desk with a party man vouching for newcomers dramatizes Tammany's citizenship help.

## Central Park
- **The El to the park:** the **Sixth Avenue El to 58th Street** stopped at the park's SW corner. The Third Ave El stopped at 59th & Third, four avenues east.
- **Bethesda Terrace:** olive-buff sandstone. The *Angel of the Waters* (1873) is brown bronze.
- **The Mall:** elms about 40–50 ft tall, the canopy not yet closed.
- **Lamps:** sparse gas posts; the Bacon lampposts came in 1907.
- **Bethesda Terrace:** a grand stair runs down from the end of the Mall to a lower terrace, with an arcade under the drive. The fountain stands on the lower terrace and the Lake lies beyond. The game shows the view from the top of the stair, over the balustrade.
- **The Mall bandstand (1862):** a painted cast-iron pavilion beside the Mall, used for Sunday concerts until it was removed in 1923.
- **Literary Walk:** statues of **Shakespeare and Sir Walter Scott**, both unveiled in 1872, at the south end of the Mall.
- **Grounds:** wooden-slat benches and rustic cedar shelters, small "Keep off the grass" boards, and **sheep on the Sheep Meadow**.

## How the game simplifies
- Building widths and heights follow typical 25-ft lots, not the actual lot-by-lot records in the 1885 Robinson atlas.
- The Statue of Liberty is placed at its true compass bearing (SW) but drawn about 2× larger than its real apparent size from the Battery, so students can photograph it.
- **Distances:** the street grid keeps the real **order and widths** of streets, but blocks are shortened to about 30 m. Mott Street is moved one block west of the Bowery (really about 6 blocks).
- **The Neighborhood Guild** is one block north of Grand, not three.
- **One ship** carries Jewish, Italian, and German passengers together for storytelling.
- **Plunkitt's bootblack-stand "office"** is documented for about 1899–1904. He used the County Courthouse long before that.
